Practice What we are doing on the ground

Fourteen live programmes, from a compulsory freshman design course to a two-day workshop for workers over 50 — each paired with a takeaway a school can act on without new budget or new tools.

C1 · CurriculumUndergraduates

Grading the Choice, Not Just the Work: 500 freshmen learn when to refuse AI

~500 Year 1 engineering and architecture students · compulsory 13-week design course

Most schools police AI (detection, bans) or promote it as a time-saver — both boil down to "did the student use AI?" Here the decision itself is graded: at every project stage each team states whether AI acted as a tool (routine work), a teammate (debating and stress-testing ideas), or was deliberately not used — and explains why. Students went further than we expected: some ran the same brief through four AI models and treated disagreement as a warning sign to investigate; others switched AI off during user interviews to protect empathy and authenticity. Teams kept reflection logs, received small credits for premium AI subscriptions, and could enter a Design·AI competition.

TakeawayAdd one line to any assignment — "state what role AI played and why". No budget, no new tools, one term — and teachers get something meaningful to mark even when the product alone no longer reveals how it was made.

C1 · CurriculumMid-career & senior professionals

Second Acts, Designed with AI: 500 older workers reimagine their careers

"What's Next" · ~500 PMETs aged 50+ · with Workforce Singapore

Conventional career guidance is one-way advice, standard skills-matching, or a generic AI demo — reflection, skills and AI kept separate. Here everything is learning-by-doing: in a two-day workshop, participants use visual maps and card games (Journey Map, Career Quest, Skill Swap) plus a purpose-built AI recommender to find skills that transfer, then get step-by-step guidance to build their own AI assistant for a real challenge in their work or life — and leave with a personal career plan. People do not sit through a talk about AI: their own decades of experience are the raw material, and building something personally useful is what changes minds.

TakeawayThe same design works for teacher PD: teachers reflect on their strengths, then build a working AI helper for a genuine school problem in the same session.

C2 · CreationPostgraduates

From Class Project to National Tool: six postgrad students build a tool adopted by the National Archives

Master's students, no coding background · adopted by the National Archives' Oral History Centre

Interviewing is typically taught through frameworks and occasional role-plays — students can describe good practice long before they can do it. Here students built the coach: "Interview Companion" reads your transcript and gives feedback, listens live and coaches you, or plays the interviewee with different personalities. To teach a skill to an AI, you first have to pin down what "good" looks like — so the builders learned the most. Released free, it was adopted by the National Archives, who built their own extended version covering their entire oral-history workflow within three months. Once built, practice became unlimited — any student can rehearse, any time, with feedback.

TakeawayWorks for any skill needing live practice — oral presentation, mother tongue, peer mediation. Set "build an AI that teaches this skill" as the assignment; the school keeps a reusable tool.

C2 · CreationEducators

Teachers as Toolmakers: four custom AI tutors built by instructors, no code needed

Master's research-methods course, urban planning · two instructors, four tools

Schools normally buy EdTech or use general chatbots as they come — the tool's design sits with a vendor. Here the instructors built the tutors themselves — one for each stage of research: framing a question, practising interviews with simulated personas, analysing field notes, applying design thinking — refined over the term using student journals and chat records. No programmers, no procurement — just carefully written instructions. The course's own teaching philosophy is written into the tools. Students reported sharper technique, and gave a valuable warning: when the AI summarised their data for them, they lost the deep familiarity the learning depended on.

TakeawayA subject head can build a syllabus-specific tutor in an afternoon, loaded with the school's own materials and rubrics. Decide in advance which struggles are the learning — and keep the AI away from those.

C2 · CreationITE & Poly students

D.A.R.E.: ITE students build AI solutions for their own communities

24 ITE students across colleges, 12 student mentors · community "Innovation Allies"

Student projects usually start from a teacher-written problem statement, and community engagement is a separate activity. Here the difference is ownership: students chose a real problem from their own community, recruited an "Innovation Ally" from their personal networks, and designed an AI-enabled solution for that person's actual need — like IncluTeach (adapting lessons for diverse learners) and TinyTrail (a children's reading companion) — with SUTD providing design, AI and facilitation support and 12 student mentors alongside. Students combined design thinking, teamwork and community perspectives in one project instead of three separate programmes.

TakeawayAn adaptable model for Applied Learning, CCE or student innovation: students move from using AI to applying it responsibly for real people in their own neighbourhoods.

C3 · CollaborationITE, Poly & Undergraduates

Eight Master Plans in Three Hours: AI agents join the facilitation team at JTC

~40 students from SUTD, Temasek Polytechnic and ITE College East · eight mixed teams · Changi Business Park

Participatory planning normally takes weeks, needs prior technical training, and scales only by hiring facilitators; AI, if used, renders decisions already made. Here the AI was inside the process: five purpose-built agents — a site expert, a design sparring partner, a panel voicing eight stakeholders, a methods coach and a critic — worked with every team at once. Students without planning backgrounds contributed from the start; all eight teams produced distinct master plans in one session, and some changed their problem statement because an AI agent, not a facilitator, challenged them. About two-thirds of teams named real-time visualisation of their spatial ideas as their main AI use, and a follow-up app, the Reimaginator, keeps recombining their ideas into hundreds of explorable options.

TakeawayTwo or three agents — a subject expert with your materials, a stakeholder role-player, a critic applying your rubric — let one teacher run rich small-group inquiry across many teams at once.

C1C3Secondary & JC

Feynman Bot for Secondary 4 Biology Revision at Raffles Girls' School

Raffles Girls' School · full Secondary 4 level

Feynman Bot is being implemented as a Biology revision support tool for the full Secondary 4 level. Students use the bot to revise teacher-selected Biology topics by explaining concepts in their own words, identifying gaps in understanding, and receiving immediate feedback within a structured revision flow. The implementation is designed around normal classroom and revision routines and is used to understand how the bot supports conceptual clarity, confidence, and self-regulated learning at cohort scale.

TakeawayEducational innovation works best when teachers, schools, and researchers co-design, implement, and refine interdisciplinary learning experiences together.

C1 · CurriculumEducators

Building Teachers' Design Mindsets for Interdisciplinary Creative Problem Solving (ICPS-D)

Annual workshop series with continued school follow-up · 76 teachers from 19 MOE schools · SUTD–NIE collaboration

Teacher professional development often focuses on content knowledge. Interdisciplinary problem solving asks more of educators — design mindsets, facilitation skills, and confidence leading open-ended inquiry. Through ICPS-D, teachers engage in hands-on design thinking, interdisciplinary project planning, and AI-assisted pedagogical approaches. Across participating schools, teachers reported stronger facilitation confidence and notable gains in design mindsets related to ambiguity, risk-taking, collaboration, and creative problem solving.

TakeawayAI alone does not transform education. Teachers need the skills, mindsets, and confidence to facilitate interdisciplinary creative problem solving — and to make meaningful use of AI in learning.

C2C3Educators

Co-Designing Interdisciplinary Projects with AI: From Planning Burden to Pedagogical Design

IDPplanner · 33 MOE teachers · Raffles Girls' School implementation · IEEE TALE 2025 · journal paper under review, 2026

Designing interdisciplinary projects is time-consuming and demands broad subject expertise: aligning curriculum objectives, integrating subjects, sequencing learning, embedding design thinking, and building assessments. IDPplanner is a GPT-based planning assistant grounded in SUTD's Design Innovation methodology, the MOE syllabus, and 21st Century Competencies. In a study with 33 in-service teachers, AI-assisted project plans showed stronger curriculum alignment, design-thinking integration, and coherence than manually developed plans. Teachers described the tool as a planning partner that sped up idea generation while keeping teacher ownership, contextual adaptation, and professional judgement intact.

TakeawayAI can act as a curriculum design partner — helping teachers build stronger interdisciplinary lessons faster, without displacing their professional judgement and contextual expertise.

C2C3Educators & students

Making Student Thinking Visible: AI-Assisted Feedback and Learning Analytics at Scale

Project Andy · 300+ students · 10 schools · Primary through postgraduate levels

Classrooms generate hundreds of student artefacts and reflections, but teachers usually see only the final submission — making it hard to spot misconceptions or track how thinking develops. Project Andy is an AI-assisted learning platform, integrated with FigJam, that captures student work as it develops, gives evidence-grounded formative feedback, and generates dimension-level scores and cohort analytics. Rather than replacing teachers, Andy helps them spot learning patterns, participation trends, and gaps across a class. Implemented across primary, secondary, junior college, and tertiary settings, its continuing school partnerships are also helping shape how AI can support curriculum innovation and project-based learning more broadly.

TakeawayValuable learning is often hard to observe directly. AI can help teachers see how students think and revise — not just what they submit — supporting timely, evidence-informed teaching decisions.

C1C3Educators, Undergraduates

Design·AI Education Guidelines for Responsible Curriculum Innovation

Practical guidance for faculty designing courses and for students

The Design·AI Education Guidelines translate SUTD's Design·AI Education framework into practical guidance for both faculty designing courses and students seeking to understand the rationale behind their curriculum. The guidelines position students as "trilingual" learners who develop domain knowledge, design methodologies, and critical AI literacy, while also foregrounding responsible use, human judgement, ethical reasoning, and innovation for real impact. They further offer assessment recommendations that help instructors design learning experiences where AI deepens rather than replaces learning, including multi-layered assessment, process-oriented evidence, explicit guidance on ethical AI use, authentic challenges that require human creativity, and intentional no-AI tasks when appropriate.

C1C3Postgraduates

iDeAL: A Postgraduate Elective in Design and AI Learning

Innovating with Design and AI for Leaders · SUTD Master's and PhD students

iDeAL is a postgraduate Design and AI elective for SUTD Master's and PhD students, extending the university's Design·AI education agenda beyond the undergraduate curriculum. The elective helps advanced learners connect design methods, AI fluency, critical judgement, and responsible innovation through hands-on exploration of AI-supported design workflows, critique, prototyping, and reflection. By engaging postgraduate students as both users and future shapers of AI-enabled design practice, iDeAL creates a space for deeper disciplinary application, research-informed experimentation, and cross-programme collaboration across SUTD's graduate community.

Undergraduate Calculus · ~550 students · planned twice-per-term deployment

Feynman Bot is planned for use in an undergraduate Calculus class serving approximately 550 students, where it will support quiz-based revision and formative practice at scale. Research What the studies tell us

Measured capability, not confidence

Each practice is paired with research — several studies supported by MOE's Tertiary Education Research Fund. One pattern runs through it all: where most studies measure whether people used AI and how confident they feel, ours measure what people can actually do — the quality of their prompts, the reliability of an AI marker, whether professionals genuinely adopt AI afterwards — and where today's assessment goes blind.

C1 · CurriculumUndergraduates

Two Hours to Better Prompts: AI fluency measured by behaviour, not self-report

434 first-years · peer-led workshop · MOE-supported · AI for Education Conf. 2026

A two-hour workshop run by trained student peer-instructors: attempt a task cold, a ten-minute lesson, fifty minutes hands-on, then reflection. Instead of asking students how confident they felt, we scored their actual written prompts before and after. Quality improved — a 15% gain, a medium-to-large effect (M = 3.00 → 3.45 on a 5-point rubric; r = .49) — on all five rubric dimensions, biggest in specifying constraints, regardless of prior AI experience.

TakeawayShort, peer-led and hands-on is a low-cost, equitable route to AI fluency — and it can be assessed from what students actually do, not confidence surveys.

C1 · CurriculumEducators

Can AI Grade Creative Work? Yes — once it's calibrated like a human marker

Year 1 design projects · Int. J. of Technology and Design Education 36(2)

The starting point was sobering: a generic AI agreed poorly with instructors — and the two human instructors barely agreed with each other. Two ingredients fixed it: the instructors' actual rubric, plus one example both rated as strong. That pushed the AI's agreement above the level expected of a second marker in high-stakes exams, and its scores stayed stable weeks apart. The unexpected benefit was diagnostic: building the AI marker exposed how differently two experienced colleagues had been reading the same rubric.

TakeawayThe move is not "let AI mark" — it's that calibrating one forces a department to state precisely what it rewards. Run it as a moderation exercise, without AI touching a live grade.

C1 · CurriculumUndergraduates

The Evidence Behind Teaching AI Refusal

Two published studies of the tool / teammate / neither approach · IEEE TALE 2025 · EDULEARN 2025

When the role decision is graded, students engage critically, not passively: cross-checking one brief across four AI models and treating disagreement as a signal to investigate; refusing AI during empathy work; reinvesting saved hours into deeper user research. One team trusted an AI-generated parts list, bought incompatible components, and learned — cheaply — to check AI advice against experts and testing. A companion study found scaffolding matters: teams improving a pre-built starter GPT progressed faster than the team starting from a blank page.

TakeawayChoosing when to use, question or refuse AI is teachable and markable. Give students a starter tool, clear roles and a reflection rubric — access alone is not a strategy.

C2 · CreationPostgraduates

What Changes When Students Build the AI Instead of Using It

Master's smart-city courses · IEEE TALE 2025 · EDULEARN 2026

Building transformed students' relationship with the technology: ownership ("it was our human experience we put in"), confidence, and sharper scepticism — "knowing the back end makes me more critical about what I see as a front-end user." The caution: quick fluency with AI tools did not equal good solutions. The deepest learning happened at moments of friction — when a plausible AI answer collided with real-world rules, stakeholders or site constraints, and students had to resolve it with their subject knowledge.

TakeawayBuilding is where the learning is. The friction between AI's confident answer and reality is the teachable moment — design tasks so students hit it.

C2 · CreationUndergraduates

The Team That Fooled the Graders: what polished work no longer proves

Comparative case study of two design teams in the same course · chapter in review

Two teams both called AI their "teammate" — with opposite realities. One argued with it, cross-checked several models, and iterated. The other had visibly weak collaboration, members largely absent — yet used AI for ideas, scripts, visuals and rubric-checking, and its polished submission was scored highly by four independent faculty markers. The lesson: AI can now paper over missing teamwork so well that a polished product no longer tells you whether the learning happened.

TakeawayIf polished work can no longer prove learning, assess the process: a short reflection, one AI exchange that changed the design, one decision-log entry showing who argued for what — a paragraph per student, and more telling than any detection software.

C2 · CreationMid-career professionals

Why Professionals Actually Adopt AI: building beats watching

112 professionals, five cohorts (2025) · plus two data collections in 2026, 19 cohorts, 420 participants

In a one-day hands-on training, professionals built AI applications — including custom chatbots — for genuine problems from their own workplaces. They showed large gains in perceived usefulness, the belief that most strongly predicts real adoption and the one demonstrations rarely move, and shifted from seeing AI as a tool to a collaborative partner. Across the two 2026 collections — government, defense, aviation, energy, healthcare, logistics — AI proficiency rose 30%, nearly double the gain of typical professional training (Round 1, without Claude Cowork: d = 1.13; Round 2, with: d = 1.08; vs d = 0.90 across 64 published AI-training studies).

TakeawayHave teachers build something for their own classroom during PD — a marking helper, a lesson-differentiation tool, a parent-communication drafter. One well-designed day is enough.

C1 · CurriculumMid-career & senior professionals

The Bottleneck Isn't AI Skills — It's Judgement

107 professionals · six workshops across public sector, engineering, urban policy, security, workforce and digital policy

We compared what participants wrote in their own words before and after each workshop. Their thinking visibly matured — from "AI automates tasks" to "AI generates options I must check against what I know about my field". Most improved on the individual skills: 93% in AI literacy, 88% in design thinking, 83% in applying their own expertise. But the hardest skill — combining all three to judge whether an AI answer is right for a real decision — moved least, at 79%. That judgement, not tool skills, is the bottleneck between AI output and good decisions.

TakeawayAim training — for staff and students — at the combination: frame the problem, check AI output against subject knowledge, decide what to act on. Tool familiarity comes quickly; judgement needs deliberate teaching.

C1C2C3Undergraduates

One Semester of AI-Embedded Design: what a full course of AI actually builds

First-year cohort · before-and-after study · MOE-supported · AI for Education Conf. 2026

AI was woven through an entire course — research, idea generation, prototyping — with explicit teaching of when to rely on AI, when to verify, when to pause. AI proficiency rose — a 7% gain, a small-to-moderate effect that is meaningful for a single course (M = 3.35 → 3.58; d = .30) — biggest in AI-supported prototyping, spotting ethical issues, and using AI to support one's own learning; originality, efficiency and emotional resilience also improved. An honest headline: the gains were real but selective — they depended on deliberate curriculum design sustained over a semester, not brief tool exposure.

TakeawayAI-integrated learning doesn't automatically improve everything. Explicitly teaching when not to use AI directly answers the biggest fear: that AI erodes independent thinking.

C1 · CurriculumEducators

Transparent Tutoring, Not a Black Box: ES-LLMs Makes Every Pedagogical Decision Inspectable

AIED 2026 full paper · 2,400 simulated learner interactions · public sandbox and code

Most AI tutors ask a single language model both to decide what to teach and to generate the response — making pedagogical choices hard to inspect. The research team's ES-LLMs architecture instead separates a Bayesian Knowledge Tracing learner model and a rule-based teaching policy from the language model that produces the response. Across 2,400 simulated interactions, this design met the tested teaching constraints in every case, while also reducing computing cost by 54% and response time by 22%. Athena-ES, an early working prototype, extends this into a downloadable, locally run tutoring assistant that shows the learner's progress, the teaching action taken, the approved source, and the final response.

TakeawayAI tutors can be designed so educators can inspect and govern teaching decisions, rather than treating a fluent answer as proof of sound pedagogy.

C2 · CreationEducators

From Classroom Need to Governed App: Educators Describe It, PedOS Makes It Reviewable

LearnAdapt · PedOS 1.1 (Lumina) · Agentic Studio · AIED 2026 Interactive Events Track

LearnAdapt is SUTD's evidence-first educational AI ecosystem, connecting adaptive learning, research trials, and governed deployment. Within it, Agentic Studio lets educators turn a plain-language classroom brief into an installable app, while PedOS governs what gets released — applying preflight checks, sandboxing, data-boundary safeguards, and human review before anything reaches students. This moves educators from consumers of fixed EdTech or generic chatbots to authors who retain authority over pedagogy, evidence, and learner data.

TakeawayTeachers should be able to build useful AI tools without giving up control over educational purpose, evidence, or learner data.

C3 · CollaborationUndergraduatesEducators

Human and AI Judgement in the Same Loop: Critique, Tutoring, and Oral Assessment

Critsly · Archie Math · CALLIOPE · working and pilot prototypes

Critsly makes AI and peer critique artefact-aware by tying feedback to learners' actual boards and images, then structuring reflection, action plans and educator analytics. Archie Math is a conversational, Socratic mathematics tutor with voice, multilingual and stylus support; it gives progressive hints rather than prematurely revealing answers. CALLIOPE is a source-grounded spoken-assessment system that shows educators the transcript and six AI-drafted evaluation dimensions for adjustment and approval; AI scores are not the sole basis for consequential grading. Archie Math and CALLIOPE are being co-designed with SUTD students.

TakeawayPut AI inside a visible, collaborative process where learners, peers, and educators can inspect, contest, and approve its outputs.

C1 · CurriculumEducators

Human and AI Assessment in the Same Loop: Supporting Creative Assessment at Scale

AI Grader · Design Thinking Assessment · MOE Teacher Study · Best Paper Award, IEEE TALE 2025

As schools adopt more open-ended, project-based learning, assessing creative work consistently becomes harder. This research explored how AI-assisted assessment can support evaluation of design-thinking artefacts that combine visual and textual elements. Teachers compared instructor, teaching-assistant, AI-generated, and hybrid assessments, and valued combining computational consistency with human judgement. AI proved most useful as a formative assessment partner — supporting feedback, reflection, and self-directed improvement — while consequential decisions stayed with educators.

TakeawayAI and educators can work together to give more timely, scalable, and meaningful feedback, while professional judgement stays at the centre of assessment.

Emerging initiativeSecondary & JC

21CC COMPASS: AI-Assisted Assessment of 21st Century Competencies

ERFP proposal currently under MOE review · SUTD–NIE–MOE collaboration, with 4 MOE schools

21CC COMPASS aims to build a competency- and process-based framework for AI-assisted assessment of open-ended projects in secondary schools, developed in collaboration with four MOE schools and the MOE office. The framework builds on and is designed to enhance MOE's existing 21st Century Competencies framework, offering an AI-amenable approach that fits within the MOE system. Building on lessons from ICPS-D and Project Andy, it connects competency frameworks, evidence-centred design, teacher professional development, and AI-assisted assessment to help educators better understand student growth in communication, collaboration, critical thinking, creativity, judgement, and other future-ready competencies.

TakeawayFuture assessment should look not only at what students produce, but at the competencies, thinking, and learning journeys behind meaningful growth.
